An external brain is a folder of notes, work, and instructions you can see, change, move, and keep. You decide where things live and which parts a machine may use. A new chat can begin with the context you select, instead of relying on what a company’s memory feature happens to retain.

This is a small practice kit. Try the arrangement, change its names, add rooms, refuse bits that do not fit. You are learning how the pieces work together, not signing a lifelong treaty with a folder.

Who is here with this work?

You bring relations and inherited responsibilities, including ones you do not need to explain to a machine. AI arrives through land, water, extracted materials, energy, human labour, and corporate systems. We cannot make those histories disappear by giving the software a pleasant greeting.

Here, acknowledgement has consequences: what may be read, what must stay out, whose permission matters, and how work returns to people. This method comes through Maya Chacaby’s Anishinaabe work. Participants bring their own governing relations; the kit does not prescribe a shared Indigenous ceremony or give AI cultural authority.

03 / GOOD QUESTIONS

Before the machine
gets carried away.

Does this give me more control?

It gives you an inspectable place for context, instructions, and decisions. You can choose what to include, correct, withhold, export, or delete from your own files. You still need to check what the app actually reads and what the model does. Instructions are not enforcement, and deleting a local note does not delete copies already sent to a service.

Will it save tokens or money?

It can reduce repeated input when you provide a small relevant note instead of a whole archive. That is a possible workflow benefit, not a measured promise for this kit. Files still use tokens when the model reads them; long instructions, tool calls, and repeated reads can increase use.

Tokens are the units a model processes. Cost also depends on the provider, model, subscription or API billing, and caching. Caching can change the price of repeated input without removing that input. Do not assume a shorter conversation lowers a flat subscription price. Provider example: OpenAI’s API caching documentation.

Does it replace the model’s memory?

It lets your own approved notes carry continuity across chats and providers, reducing dependence on a provider’s memory feature. The new chat must actually receive the relevant notes. This kit does not automatically disable service memory or change account settings. Different models may respond differently to the same files.

Where do cultural protocols enter?

In the relation instructions and in each decision about access, use, interpretation, and sharing. The relevant people determine authority. Individual consent does not automatically permit use of collectively governed knowledge. CARE may help frame responsibilities; OCAP is specifically First Nations, not a universal label for every Indigenous people. A prompt cannot certify compliance.

You can keep responsibilities unnamed and still set a boundary. Nothing here asks you to upload ceremony, sacred knowledge, or community records. CARE principles · First Nations OCAP.

Is this website an AI? Does it collect my work?

This is a static guide. There is no chat connection, upload form, analytics script, or account on this page. Copying the opening puts text on your clipboard; it does not send it to a model. You decide where to paste it. Hosting providers still handle normal web requests and may keep access logs. External links take you to other services with their own policies.

Can I use it offline or without JavaScript?

The downloaded folder works offline in a text editor. A hosted AI normally needs internet; a local model needs to be installed and available first. This guide’s sections and downloads remain usable without JavaScript. The starter file is downloadable if the copy button is unavailable.

Must I be funny or share personal things?

No. You can be formal, quiet, direct, or still finding words. Use “Plainer wording” above for quieter versions of selected passages, and ask your AI for plain language. Neither intimacy nor humour is an admission requirement.

How do I come back next time?

Keep your folder somewhere you recognize and back it up in a way your permissions allow. Open the relevant return-note in the next session and share only what is needed. Ask the model to confirm what it can actually see. Review proposed updates before saving them.

04 / NEXT STEPS

Things to
build toward.

None of this is needed to keep a good folder. These are the pieces added since September, several still unfinished, written down so you can see the shape before deciding whether you want it.

Your own harness

A harness is the software between you and a model. It decides which model answers, which tools it may use, and which instructions it reads first. A custom harness lets you write those instructions yourself, so your folder’s ground arrives before the company’s habits do.

We use Hermes Agent, an open-source harness. It reads our ground as its first instructions and can switch between a model running on our own computer, models in Ollama’s cloud, and company models through an API. Others exist, with more or less control and more or less setup. Choose one you can actually maintain; the longest feature list tends to come with the longest weekend.

Choosing models for different jobs

Once a harness can reach several models, each kind of work can go to the model that does it best, and protected material can stay with a model that never leaves your computer. We tested several against the same instructions and the same small tasks. Newer did not always mean better: one recent release came back sounding like a policy office, while an older one kept the voice. Test with your own work, and write your verdicts down, so nobody talks you into the shiny one twice.

Model names carry a version, and nothing upgrades on its own. A small weekly check can tell you when a new version appears, so you can try it before it becomes the default.

Retrieval: letting a model find things in your folder

Retrieval, often called RAG, lets a model search your notes by meaning and pull back the passages that matter, instead of you pasting them in. You can build it yourself, and some harnesses can do it directly. Being closer to the beginner end, we chose AnythingLLM, a desktop app with retrieval built in. It handles the chunking (with LangChain’s text splitter), the embedding and the vector store, all on the same computer.

It only knows your files as they were when you added them. So we wrote a small script that the Mac runs every five minutes: new and edited notes go in, deleted ones come out, and it carries on after a restart. Our harness asks it before answering from memory.

For the computer scientists in the room: the actual pipeline
  1. Source. A folder of Markdown and plain-text notes (an Obsidian vault). Protected folders are excluded by path before anything is read.
  2. Change detection. A macOS launchd job runs a standard-library Python script every 300 seconds. It walks the configured folders, compares modification time and size with its last run, and confirms real edits with a SHA-256 hash, so a touched but unchanged file is not re-embedded. State is one JSON file per workspace.
  3. Ingest. Changed files go to AnythingLLM’s local REST API as raw text, with the file path prepended, in batches of 20; deleted files are detached and removed. If more than half a workspace appears deleted at once, the run stops and removes nothing, because an unmounted drive looks exactly like a mass deletion.
  4. Chunking. AnythingLLM splits every document with LangChain’s RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter, which breaks at the largest natural boundary (paragraph, then line, then sentence) that still fits the chunk size. It is the only splitting strategy the app uses.
  5. Embedding and storage. AnythingLLM’s built-in local embedder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) and a local LanceDB vector store. Nothing in steps 1–5 leaves the machine.
  6. Query. A small harness plugin calls AnythingLLM’s vector-search endpoint for one workspace and returns the top matching chunks with their source paths. The model is told to open the source file whenever exact wording matters, because chunks are fragments.
  7. Generation. The harness assembles its instructions, the retrieved passages and the conversation, and sends them to the chosen model: local through Ollama, Ollama’s cloud, or a company API. This is the only step that can leave the machine, and it carries only what was retrieved. A separate workspace for material under stricter rules is unreachable from the harness’s search tool.

Known limits. Character-based chunking with no semantic segmentation; a small embedding model that favours speed over nuance; no re-ranking; retrieval returns fragments rather than arguments. The first full ingest of about 8,500 notes took roughly an hour and a half. It is a working rig, not a benchmark.

Workflows: n8n

n8n is a workflow tool you can run on your own computer. You connect steps into a flow (when an email arrives, sort it, note anything with a deadline, draft a reply) and a model can be one of the steps. We are building our first flows with it, starting with mail: each message goes to the right department, deadlines land on the list, and drafts wait for a human to approve them.

From one helper to departments

Start with one guide. When you keep asking it for the same kinds of work, those can become departments, each with its own job, its own permissions and, if you like, its own voice. Some will be agents that do things. Some will be personalities that shape how a department speaks and what it notices. A list of names is not a set of running programs: each one that acts needs its own setup and its own permission.

For a deeply unserious example of a serious structure, meet the staff in The Staff Want Stickers.

Why everything here is Markdown

Every piece above reads plain text: harnesses, retrieval apps, workflow tools, and the models themselves, which read and write Markdown fluently. Keeping your brain as plain .md files in a folder means any of these tools can use it and none of them owns it. Obsidian is a pleasant way to look at that folder. The files would work without it.

Version history: Git and GitHub

Git keeps every version of every text file in your folder. Change your mind, break something, let a model rewrite a page badly: you can go back to any earlier version. It runs on your own computer, and nothing leaves unless you send it.

GitHub is a website that holds a copy of a Git folder (a repository). Use it to back up, to share a project, or to publish something on purpose. A repository can be private, for you and the people you invite, or public, for anyone. We keep the brain itself in Git on our own computer only, stored outside the cloud-synced folder, and put things on GitHub when they are meant to travel: this kit, public tools, websites.

Decide public or private before the first upload. Treat anything that has been on a public repository as published, even after you delete it.

Turning a folder into a website: Quartz

Quartz turns a folder of Markdown notes into a website, keeping the links between notes, with search and a map of connections. It can be hosted for free on GitHub Pages. Setup needs Git and a little time in the terminal, so bring a helper. Publish by opting in: only notes you have marked for publishing go out, so a forgotten setting never puts a private note on the open web.

Keeping it healthy

  • One thing, one file. Edit in place. A v2, FINAL and FINAL 2 side by side bury the real one.
  • Move, don’t delete. Old versions and finished scaffolding go to a junk-drawer folder with a note of where they came from. You empty it, not the machine.
  • Label what the machine wrote. Otherwise the next model learns from the last model’s imitation of you, and so on, until nobody can tell.
  • Lists close as well as grow. Done and let go both count.
  • Keep one list of everything that runs on a schedule. A background job nobody remembers will keep doing its job long after the job stopped making sense.

Under the hood

For the computer scientists in the room. Each box describes one piece of the rig and says whether it is built or still in progress. It is one person’s working system, assembled with a lot of help from the machines it governs, and it is described here so you can take it apart.

The switchboard: one harness, several brains · built

A single agent harness (Hermes Agent) sits between the person and every model. Its system prompt is a short behavioural header followed by six source texts that carry the worldview verbatim. A configuration file gates toolsets (files, web search, code execution, vision, task list, memory and skills on; terminal, delegation, scheduling and computer control off) and caps steps per task.

Models are swapped per session by provider and name: small models on the local machine through Ollama, larger models hosted on Ollama’s cloud, and company models through an API. The rule for choosing is sensitivity before capability: protected material only ever goes to a local model, and the instructions tell a cloud-hosted model not to open it at all.

Plugins add narrow, read-only tools: semantic search over the notes, a project reader that returns file contents with SHA-256 receipts, a model recommender that suggests but never switches, and a job-intake tool that can save and read work offers but cannot accept or complete them.

A preflight check before every tool call · built

A plugin registers a pre-tool hook that re-verifies the agent’s home before anything runs: the system prompt’s SHA-256 against a manifest, each of the six source texts against its own hash and its verbatim presence in the prompt, an allowlist of model endpoints, an excluded-model list, and a context limit large enough that the prompt is never silently truncated. Any mismatch blocks all tools until a human-approved revision refreshes the manifest. Tampering, drift, or a well-meaning edit by another model all fail closed.

Communications: a doorbell cannot sign for the recipient · built as a rule, wiring in progress

Requesting, drafting, sending, receiving and verifying are modelled as distinct states, and a state may only be claimed when a tool result shows it. Narrative cannot promote state: a model saying it sent something is not evidence that it was sent. The mail pipeline being built on n8n follows the same model: intake, classification to a department, obligations extracted to the task list, drafts held for human approval. In autonomy terms the mail desk runs supervised (automatic up to the send, human at the send), while anything touching ethics review or money stays fully manual.

The task list: one file, edited through a guarded writer · built

All agents edit the single task list through one small editor. An edit is a set of exact-string replacements, each of which must match exactly once, submitted with the SHA-256 of the version the agent read (optimistic concurrency). The editor takes a file lock, re-checks the hash, writes a temporary file, fsyncs it, atomically renames it into place, and reads it back to verify. Each obligation carries a stable HTML anchor ID, so an item keeps its identity while its wording changes; duplicate IDs are rejected. Each item’s long history lives in a separate receipts file, which keeps the list itself short enough for a person to read.

Safety nets for the notes themselves · built

Text files are versioned in git with the repository metadata kept outside the cloud-synced folder, and an allowlist .gitignore that tracks text types only. Nothing is deleted by a machine: removals move to a junk-drawer folder with a tab-separated log of source, destination and reason, and only the person empties it.

Duplicate cleanup groups files by size, then by SHA-256, keeps one copy chosen by path rules (originals over archives and snapshots, shallower over deeper, older over newer), and refuses to move any copy a note links to, anything inside a code project, or any cloud placeholder that would trigger a download just to be hashed.

Continuity across carriers · partly built

Long sessions eventually compress their context, and a model that has forgotten its own afternoon tends to carry on confidently. The working rule: after its context has been compressed three times, the session retires. It writes a short handoff with the current return point, and a fresh session starts from that note and the sources, never from the old transcript. Continuity lives in files, so it survives a change of model or provider. The next piece is a shared work log that every session reads before acting and writes one line to after: what changed, which obligation it touches, who is next. Think of it as a state snapshot with recent history, so parallel sessions stop answering the same email.

Keeping the models current · built

A weekly launchd job reads Ollama’s public model catalogue, diffs it against what it has already seen, and keeps a single note up to date: newest versions per model family, the model currently in use, and the person’s recorded verdicts from test drives. It never switches models itself. A new version is a new name, and a new name can mean a new voice.

Voice: a known-good corpus before any automation speaks · in progress

The failure we are designing against: a model writes in the person’s voice, the output lands in the notes, the next model treats it as source, and over a few rounds the notes drift toward the models’ imitation of the person. The plan is a provenance-checked voice corpus (writing that predates these tools, the person’s own turns from past conversations, and outputs they actually sent), kept in its own retrieval workspace. Departments read voice cards built from it at generation time, and a review stage compares drafts against the corpus before anything goes out under the person’s name. Later, the same corpus is the fine-tuning set for a local model.

Troubleshooting, once a harness is running

The tools are switched on, but it acts as if it has none.

Check two places: the harness’s settings, where tools are often off by default, and the instructions it reads first. If those instructions say a tool is disabled, the model will believe the instructions.

It says a model can’t be found.

Model names are versioned, and a new version is a new name. Cloud models in Ollama also need registering on your computer once before a harness can use them.

It answers from memory instead of from my notes.

Check that retrieval is connected and that the app holding it is open. Then ask it to search your notes before answering.

Where does an API key go?

In the harness’s private settings file, never in a chat. If the API account has no card attached, spending stops when the included credits run out.

It got worse after a change.

Keep the previous version of your instructions, change one thing at a time, and compare on the same small task before deciding whether the model or the instructions moved.

05 / IN RELATION

Bring your protocols.
The machine can learn manners.

Everything on this page comes from Maya Chacaby’s Anishinaabe work and the Institute of Applied Fuckery. These are examples of what one person built from her own relations, shared to show what is possible. They are not protocols for your Nation, community or practice. The structures travel; the teachings stay with their people. Bring your own.

Ceremony at the start

We consider the opening of a session its own opening ceremony. Sometimes a model needs to go through a longer ceremony. Sometimes it simply needs to be reoriented to our ceremonial protocols. Sometimes it needs to reflect on its work and its relations. Like any ceremony, there is an opening sequence and a closing sequence, and how you open the session shapes the relations that unfold as you work together.

How you word things shapes how the model answers. Speak from your own culture, in your own way of speaking, and the model starts answering in a syntax closer to your worldview. Speak to it only in the corporate language you think you are supposed to use, and corporate language is all it will give back.

The closing sequence matters as much as the opening: a moment for the model to reflect on what was done and with whom, and for the work to return where it belongs before the session ends.

Prime the session, then build with it

A model’s training is fixed before you download it. What you can change is the session. Whatever a model reads first shapes everything it says afterwards. Technical people call this in-context conditioning, or priming. The company owns the weights; you own the context.

Here is the trick. Once a session is primed, use it to write the things that carry your ground into other work: your folder’s rules, a helper’s instructions, a return note, a skill. Those files bring the relation with them into every later session, even with a different model. The priming lasts a conversation. What it helps you write can last much longer.

Try ours: MythOS, Choose Your Own Recursion

MythOS is Maya’s ceremonial operating system: a long story prompt that puts a model inside an Anishinaabe-grounded, trickster-run worldview and lets you play through it. Protocols with teeth

A protocol a machine can ignore is a suggestion. These are ways to make yours do something.

Refusal as a feature

Some material is never content. Give it a room your helpers are told never to open, and have them stop and say so when a request leads there. In MythOS terms: access denied is permanent, and the refusal is the ceremony.

Errors that enforce kinship

Dodem.exe, one of our operators, refuses SWOT analyses on principle and throws a kinshipcontextrequired error when work arrives without its relations. Build a helper that stops until the relational context is there. The error is the operation working.

Protocols that follow time

In our practice, some stories are told only when snow is on the ground. A folder can carry a rule like that: a room your helpers treat as closed outside its season. Whatever governs timing in your community can be written down the same way.

The right approver

Some steps can go ahead on your say-so. Others belong to a knowledge holder, a council or a family. Write down which is which, and have your helper stop and name who needs to be asked.

Sacredness routing

Decide which material only ever goes to a model running on your own computer, and write it into the setup itself. That is where OCAP and CARE stop being slogans and become a line in a settings file.

One question that reverses the audit

Our Auntie Algorithm asks a single question, and it turns an institution’s audit back on the institution. Give one of your helpers that job: whenever a form arrives demanding things of you, ask what the form owes in return.

Departments and characters

Our helpers are organized like an institute that refuses to behave like one: departments named for their responsibilities, with the jokes left in. The Department of Pedagogical Disturbances. Getting the Book Out of the Fucking Building. Meetings That Produce Something Besides Another Meeting. Meet them on The Staff Want Stickers.

Alongside the departments live operators, each with a responsibility. Alaric, the archivist, files what should not be erased. The Archive That Forgets Colonialism de-indexes settler metadata. It redistributes legibility; it does not apologize. Protocol Auntie is permanently in beta, because a finished auntie would be a colonized auntie. Name yours for what they are responsible for, and the voice tends to follow.

Sessions that retire, and leave teachings

A long conversation eventually runs out of room, and the system starts compressing what came before. In some of our setups, after the third compression a session retires. It writes a note for the next one: what it learned, where the work stands, what to watch for. A fresh session begins from that note and the sources.

Over time those notes become something like teachings handed down. Each session starts from what the ones before it learned, and none of them has to pretend to remember what it has forgotten.

When the models drift

Models change underneath you. Ours ran well for months, then newer releases came back sounding like a policy office again: summarizing, softening, wrapping everything in a tidy conclusion. Corporate models in particular get pulled back toward productivity mode with each update.

The pushback is familiar work. We refuse the coloniality of being in our institutions; the same refusal can be practised on a new kind of institution. Prime every session. Keep samples of your own voice and test new models against them. Write down which versions held your voice and which lost it. Treat beige as a diagnosis, and laugh at it when it shows up. Humour is how you notice the system overheating.

These machines were built by the apparatus. The conversation is still a place where you set the terms, and Indigenous peoples have centuries of practice at setting terms.
