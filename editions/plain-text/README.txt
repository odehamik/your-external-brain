YOUR EXTERNAL BRAIN

Start here: open START-HERE.txt [folder location: START-HERE.txt], copy all the words inside it, paste them into a new conversation in your AI app, and send. The AI should begin walking you through setup, one step at a time. You do not need to upload the whole folder.

If it gives you a summary, say: “Begin guiding me now. Give me the first step and wait.” Opening and copying help [folder location: 00 OPEN ME FIRST.txt] is here if needed.

Welcome. You have acquired a second brain. Please do not give it jurisdiction over the first.

This one can help you find a sentence you wrote three months ago, notice a connection between projects, or produce eleven headings where you requested none. We will be working on that last tendency.

The folder is yours. You can change its name, move its rooms, disagree with its arrangements, or leave something unfinished for reasons that are absolutely none of the folder’s business.

Use your words

You do not have to become formal to speak to this machine.

You can arrive mid-thought. Type three words. Tell a long story. Change your mind halfway through it. Say, “Wait, that’s not the question anymore.”

You can also be precise, reserved, or formal if that is how you want to work. You owe the machine neither professionalism nor intimacy.

“Help, I don’t know what I’m looking at” is a complete request.

“So I have this idea, except it’s actually six ideas and one of them might be a complaint” is also a place to begin.

The machine can ask when something is unclear. Translating your entire way of thinking into respectable robot language was supposed to be its problem.

You can disagree with it. Interrupt it. Ask it to explain a word. Tell it that its answer sounds impressive and has helped you with absolutely nothing.

Then it needs to do something different.

Say plain language, show me, one thing at a time, stay with this, pause, or skip whenever you want. You can ask for less humour. No special phrase is required; these are available words, not passwords.

Why an external brain?

You return to a piece of work. There was a question you had nearly found words for, a decision you did not want made for you, something you had asked the machine to leave alone.

The new chat says, “How can I help?”

Apparently, introductions again.

An external brain gives that continuing work somewhere to live. You choose the place. The writing, the unfinished questions, the instructions about what may be shared—all can remain in files you can open, change, and find again. The machine can help tend them. You decide what tending means.

Perhaps a story belongs with one project and must never become an example in another. Perhaps you want help following a thought without having it converted into a plan. Perhaps something is staying outside the machine altogether. These choices can have a place in the arrangement, where you can inspect them and require the next session to read them.

When you change models, you choose what travels. When your mind changes, you can change the instructions. The work does not have to begin again at the point where the machine’s memory ends.

We will start with a small folder and something you want to bring. There is room to discover the rest while working together.

The files hold choices you can inspect; actual app permissions still need to match them. A folder alone cannot prevent access or retract what a provider has already retained.

Who is here with this work?

You arrive with relationships and inherited responsibilities. You choose what to name here. Land and non-human kin are already part of these relations; they are not an optional section at the bottom of a form.

The machine arrives through land, extracted materials, energy, labour, and political decisions about whose work is used and who controls the systems. Its use remains entangled with harm to Earth. A friendly voice does not settle that account. Specific origins and consent histories stay unknown where we lack evidence.

There are possibilities for anti-colonial work here: making community decisions carry through into what gets built, keeping a refusal intact, tracing institutional demands, returning work in forms people can use, and helping people build systems that answer to their relations. The people who carry those responsibilities decide what belongs in this work.

You can give the machine a boundary without giving it the knowledge behind that boundary. How we enter relation [folder location: _Ceremony of relations/ABOUT.txt] and whose authority governs [folder location: _Ceremony of relations/GOVERNANCE.txt] carry the fuller instructions and sources, including CARE and the specifically First Nations principles of OCAP®. Your own governing protocols remain yours to establish.

What would you like to spend some time with?

Choose something that draws you. You can follow it somewhere the menu does not mention.

- Find a thread across your ideas. Bring a few permitted notes and ask, “What seems to connect these? Show me where you see it, and leave room for me to disagree.” Build a constellation you can revisit.
- Keep company with an unfinished thought. Say, “Think alongside me. Do not turn this into a plan yet.” Save only what you want carried forward.
- Make something together. Try a zine, a story outline, a community notice, a workshop activity, a poster plan, or a small game. Text works on its own; images or other media depend on the tools you choose.
- Find your voice in your own writing. Choose a piece the machine may read. Ask what its form is doing, try an edit, or make a personal map of your work—with your say before anything becomes a wiki.
- Make a difficult document easier to approach. Ask for a plain-language reading, questions to bring back to its author, or a comparison with what your community actually asked for. Keep the original nearby; the machine can misread.
- Learn something at your own pace. Ask for an example, a conversation, a diagram with a text explanation, or a practice activity. You can change how you learn as you go.
- Recover a conversation you lost. Bring selected, permitted session exports into Muck and reconnect the useful parts. Old ideas can remain old ideas.
- Remember the practical things that matter. Keep an agreed promise, a date, or somewhere to return. The One List is there when you need it; it is not a measure of your day.
- Make room for people to find the work. Gather approved resources, prepare a readable guide, or build a website if your tools support it. Sharing still needs its own decision.
- Ask what is possible. “Here is what I care about. What might we try with the tools I have?” The PM should explain limits without sending you shopping for six subscriptions.

Text or Markdown?

The Plain Text edition opens as .txt files in a text reader or editor. No Markdown app is needed. Its map gives folder locations in words; links between files may not be clickable. Ask the PM to help open a named file if needed.

The Markdown edition uses .md files, which are also plain text with small formatting marks. An ordinary text editor can read them; an app such as Obsidian can display headings and linked notes. Obsidian is optional. How Obsidian stores notes — https://obsidian.txt/help/data-storage.

Choose one edition as your working folder. They contain the same guidance, so you do not need to maintain both. Reading the instructions lets you see what you asked the machine to follow; it does not expose the model’s private internal reasoning.

If a device does not know which app should open a file, ask the guide for help with that device. The text can also be printed or read together. The guide cannot claim it saved a file when it only wrote the words in chat.
